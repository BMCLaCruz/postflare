## When to use
- Something happened while building or running the product that taught you a lesson: a launch that flopped, a support email that changed the roadmap, a pricing mistake.
- You want founder-led reach on LinkedIn without posting an ad.
- You have a specific detail (a number, a quote, a date) that makes the story real.

Do not use it for announcements with no story, or for a lesson you cannot back with what actually happened.

## Structure
1. **Lines 1–2: the hook, before "…see more".** LinkedIn folds after ~210 characters or 3 lines on mobile. Line 1 ≤ 60 characters: a specific moment or a tension ("We lost 40% of trial users on one screen."). Line 2 adds the twist or stakes. No greeting, no "I'm excited to share".
2. **Blank line, then the story.** Short paragraphs of 1–2 lines each, blank lines between. Past tense, first person, concrete: who, where, what number.
3. **The turn.** The moment you realised what was wrong. One or two lines.
4. **The lesson.** One sentence, stated plainly. Optionally 2–3 short bullet points of what you changed.
5. **The question.** A specific question that lets readers share their own version ("What's the screen you were sure was fine?").
6. **Link in the first comment.** No link in the body; LinkedIn reduces reach for posts with external links. In Postflare, add it as a follow-up item, which becomes the first comment. Mention "link in the first comment" only if the link matters to the story.

Aim for 500–1,500 characters. The limit is 3,000, but a story that needs the full limit usually has two stories in it.

## Platform notes
- **LinkedIn personal profile (`linkedin`):** stories from a person tend to travel further than from a company page. Write as the founder, in their voice.
- **LinkedIn page (`linkedin_page`):** works for team stories ("our support lead noticed…"); keep the same structure and name the person, with permission.
- 3–5 hashtags at most, at the end, or none. Tag people only if they are in the story and agreed.
- A single image (a screenshot, a photo of the whiteboard) can help, but text-only story posts perform fine; do not add a stock photo.
- Line breaks are preserved; do not use Markdown bold, it shows as asterisks.
- Reply to early comments in the first hour; the conversation is what extends reach.

## Hooks
- **The specific failure:** "We {did thing} and {bad result with number}." — honest failure pulls more readers than success.
- **Belief reversal:** "I was sure {belief}. {Event} proved me wrong." — sets up a lesson in two lines.
- **The email/message:** "A user emailed us one line: '{quote}'." — a real voice and a mystery.
- **Small number, big change:** "{Small change} changed {metric} more than {big effort}." — counterintuitive and concrete.

## Examples
### LinkedIn personal post (Tempo founder, ~770 characters)
```
We spent 3 months building a feature only 2% of users opened.
The fix took one afternoon and wasn't a feature at all.

Tempo is a focus timer. Last spring we built "Focus Rooms":
work alongside strangers on video, timers in sync.

Beautiful. Hard to build. We were proud of it.

After launch, 2% of users opened it once.
Fewer came back.

Then a user emailed us one line:
"I just want my phone to shut up while the timer runs."

We had a "mute notifications" setting.
It was 4 taps deep in Settings.

We moved it onto the start button as a toggle.
Sessions completed went up that same week.

The lesson I keep relearning:
users rarely ask for new things.
They ask for the thing you already have, where they can reach it.

What's a feature you built that users never found?
```
First comment (follow-up): "The full write-up with the before/after screens: https://tempo.example/blog/focus-rooms"

### LinkedIn page post (Pocket Plants, ~480 characters)
```
Our support lead read 300 one-star reviews in a weekend.
Only one complaint showed up more than 50 times.

It wasn't bugs. It wasn't price.
It was "the app said water it, and it died anyway."

Our reminders were on a fixed schedule.
Plants aren't.

Lina proposed linking reminders to local weather and season.
It shipped in October.

Lesson: the most useful feedback was already public.
We just hadn't read it all in one sitting.

When did you last read every review of your product?
```

## Avoid
- "I'm humbled/excited to announce", "Agree?", "Thoughts?" as the whole question.
- One-word-per-line "broetry" and fake drama ("I was fired. Then I cried. Then…") that the facts don't support.
- External links in the body; put them in the first comment.
- Engagement pods, "comment 'guide' and I'll DM you", or asking for likes.
- Stock photos and generic AI images.
- Hashtag walls; more than 5 tags.
- Copying the LinkedIn version unchanged to X or Instagram.

## Checklist
- [ ] Lines 1–2 hook the reader within ~210 characters and contain no greeting.
- [ ] Every paragraph is 1–2 short lines with blank lines between.
- [ ] The story has at least one concrete detail (number, quote, date).
- [ ] The lesson is a single plain sentence.
- [ ] Ends with a specific question, not "Thoughts?".
- [ ] No URL in the body; any link is a follow-up (first comment).
- [ ] Under 3,000 characters, ideally 500–1,500.
- [ ] No Markdown formatting, at most 5 hashtags, people tagged only with consent.
